## The short version

This topic is about how ecologists actually measure a living system rather than just describe it: how many species are here, how many individuals of each, is that population growing or shrinking, and what's driving all of it. Every dot point below is a tool for turning "this place seems diverse" or "that population seems healthy" into a number you can defend, compare, and argue from — which is exactly what QCAA's external exam asks you to do with unseen data.

## Dot point by dot point

### Describe genetic, species and ecosystem diversity

**What it means** — Biodiversity isn't one thing, it's three nested levels. Genetic diversity is the variation in alleles *within* a species (why not all wattles are identical). Species diversity is the variety of species *within* an ecosystem (both how many, and how their numbers compare — see the two dot points below). Ecosystem diversity is the variety of habitats, communities and ecological processes across a *landscape*.

**Why it matters** — Every other dot point in this topic sits inside one of these three levels, so if you can't place a scenario correctly ("is this question about genetic, species or ecosystem diversity?"), you'll answer the wrong question even with the right content. Genetic diversity is also the raw material natural selection acts on later in Unit 4 — this is where that idea starts.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Describe* [2–3 marks]: "Distinguish between genetic diversity and species diversity, using an example." Two marks means two distinct, correctly-labelled definitions — not one long paragraph that blurs the two together.

### Describe the biological species concept and identify its limitations

**What it means** — The biological species concept (BSC) defines a species as a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring in nature. Its limitations: it can't be applied to asexual organisms (bacteria, many plants), can't be tested on fossils, and breaks down with ring species and hybrids that blur the boundary.

**Why it matters** — QCAA explicitly wants you to hold a definition *and* its limits at the same time — that's the "science as a human endeavour" habit of mind running underneath the whole subject: classification systems are useful tools, not perfect truths.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Identify* [1 mark] a limitation, then *explain* [2 marks] why it's a limitation for a specific named group (e.g. bacteria). Two separate command verbs in one question is common here — answer each one, don't merge them.

### Determine the diversity of species using measures such as species richness, evenness, percentage cover, percentage frequency and Simpson's diversity index

**What it means** — **Richness** = the number of different species present (a count). **Evenness** = how equally individuals are distributed across those species (a proportion) — a community can have high richness but low evenness if one species dominates. **Percentage cover** and **percentage frequency** are field measures from sampling (see the next dot point). **Simpson's diversity index**, $SDI = 1 - \sum \frac{n(n-1)}{N(N-1)}$, combines richness and evenness into one number: $n$ = individuals of one species, $N$ = total individuals of all species. Closer to 1 = more diverse.

**Why it matters** — This is the calculation QCAA reaches for most often in this topic because it rewards understanding *why* a formula works, not just plugging numbers in. Getting $N$ and $n$ confused, or forgetting to sum across every species before dividing, is the single most common error.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Calculate/Determine* [3–4 marks], given a small table of species counts: show the working for each species' $n(n-1)$, sum them, then complete the formula. Marks are awarded for method at each step, so show every line even if the arithmetic slips.

### Describe how sampling can be used to investigate the species diversity of a given area

**What it means** — Three layers to hold apart: **sampling method** (how you choose *where* to sample — random, systematic, stratified), **sampling technique** (the *tool* — quadrats, line transects, belt transects, capture-recapture), and **strategies to minimise bias** (sample size and number, random-number generators to avoid unconscious pattern-choosing, consistent counting criteria, calibrated equipment).

**Why it matters** — Validity and reliability questions in the external exam are almost always about this dot point: "would this sampling method give a valid/reliable result, and why/why not". You need the vocabulary exactly right — mixing up "method" and "technique" reads as not understanding either.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Justify* [3 marks]: "A student wants to sample daisies in a field with a visible gradient from shade to full sun. Justify an appropriate sampling method." The correct answer is *stratified* (because the habitat isn't uniform) with reasoning tied to the gradient — not just "random, because it avoids bias" pasted in from memory.

### Explain that ecosystems are composed of varied habitats, including microhabitats, which may impact the distribution of species, and therefore the validity and reliability of different sampling methods

**What it means** — Species can be distributed **uniformly** (evenly spaced, often from competition), **randomly** (no pattern), or **clumped** (patchy, often from resources or social behaviour). Which pattern is present determines which sampling method is actually valid — random sampling under-samples a clumped distribution unless you sample enough quadrats.

**Why it matters** — This is the *reasoning* that sits behind the previous dot point's vocabulary. QCAA distinguishes "describe the sampling toolkit" from "explain why a specific tool suits a specific distribution" — the second is worth more marks because it demands you connect two ideas rather than recall one.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Explain* [3 marks], always paired with a described scenario (never asked in the abstract): identify the distribution pattern implied by the scenario, then explain the consequence for sampling validity.

### Use the Lincoln index to estimate the size of a population

**What it means** — Capture-recapture: capture and mark $M$ individuals, release them, then later capture a second sample of $n$ individuals of which $m$ are already marked. Population estimate $N = \frac{M \times n}{m}$. Assumes: no births/deaths/migration between samples, marks don't affect survival or catchability, marked individuals redistribute randomly.

**Why it matters** — Every one of those assumptions is a potential exam question in its own right ("suggest why this estimate might be inaccurate") — the calculation is the easy half of this dot point.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Calculate* [2 marks] the population using given values, then *evaluate* [2–3 marks] the reliability of the estimate given a scenario that breaks one of the assumptions (e.g. the species is highly mobile, or marking affects predation risk).

### Calculate population growth rate and change using birth, death, immigration and emigration data

**What it means** — Population change = (births + immigration) − (deaths + emigration). Growth *rate* expresses that change relative to population size, usually per year.

**Why it matters** — This is the arithmetic foundation the growth-curve dot point below builds on conceptually — get the four terms and their signs right here first.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Calculate* [2–3 marks] from a small data table. Show each term separately before combining them; a bare final number with no working loses the method marks even if it's correct.

### Identify and explain different modes of population growth: exponential growth (J-curve); logistic growth (S-curve)

**What it means** — **Exponential (J-curve)**: growth rate proportional to population size, unrestricted by resources — theoretical, short-term, seen when a population colonises a new area with abundant resources. **Logistic (S-curve)**: growth slows as the population approaches **carrying capacity** ($K$, the maximum population an environment can sustain) due to density-dependent limiting factors (competition, disease, predation).

**Why it matters** — The exam virtually always gives you a graph and asks you to identify which curve, name the phase (lag/exponential/plateau), and explain *why* the curve bends where it does — that "why", tied to carrying capacity and limiting factors, is where the marks concentrate, not the naming.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Interpret* a given graph [2 marks], then *explain* [3 marks] why growth slows after a labelled point, naming the limiting factor(s) responsible.

### Compare the reproductive strategies and growth curves of K- and r- strategists

**What it means** — **r-strategists**: many small offspring, little parental care, short lifespan, exploit unstable/unpredictable environments, population tends toward the J-curve (e.g. insects, weeds). **K-strategists**: few large offspring, high parental investment, long lifespan, populations regulated near carrying capacity, tend toward the S-curve (e.g. elephants, humans).

**Why it matters** — "Compare" specifically means similarities *and* differences addressed against each other — not two separate paragraphs, one per strategy. This is a genuine trap: most students write two descriptions and call it a comparison.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Compare* [4 marks]: structure the answer as paired points ("r-strategists produce many offspring with little care, whereas K-strategists produce few offspring with high care") rather than "r-strategists are... [paragraph]. K-strategists are... [paragraph]."

### Describe how the distribution and abundance of species in an ecosystem are influenced by biotic and abiotic factors

**What it means** — **Biotic factors**: food availability, competition, predation, disease — factors arising from other living things. **Abiotic factors**: space, shelter, water availability, nutrients, other environmental conditions (temperature, light, pH) — non-living factors.

**Why it matters** — This dot point is the vocabulary the "interpret an experiment" dot point below is tested through — you're given real or simulated data and asked to name which biotic or abiotic factor it demonstrates.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Describe* [1 mark each] usually appears as a short-answer list item; the higher-mark version asks you to *explain* how a named factor specifically limits distribution in a given scenario.

### Interpret data from an experiment investigating how abiotic factors affect the distribution, abundance and/or biodiversity of species

**What it means** — Reading an unfamiliar dataset (a table or graph of an abiotic variable against species counts) and drawing a conclusion that's actually supported by the numbers in front of you — not general textbook knowledge about that factor.

**Why it matters** — "Interpret" means referring to the actual values and features of the data you were given. A correct general fact that never cites the data scores as an unsupported claim, not an interpretation.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Interpret* [3–4 marks]: quote specific values from the given data ("abundance fell from 40 to 12 individuals as soil moisture dropped below 15%") before stating the relationship — the numbers ARE the evidence for the mark.

### Interpret data to classify and name ecosystems using Specht's classification system and the Holdridge life zone classification scheme

**What it means** — **Specht's system** classifies Australian vegetation by the structure and density of the tallest layer (e.g. closed forest, woodland, open woodland, based on percentage crown cover). The **Holdridge life zone scheme** classifies ecosystems globally using mean annual biotemperature, precipitation and potential evapotranspiration ratio, plotted on Holdridge's triangular diagram.

**Why it matters** — Two named, specific classification systems — QCAA can ask you to apply either one to given data (e.g. "using the figures provided, classify this ecosystem under Specht's system") rather than just define them.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Interpret/Classify* [2–3 marks], given numeric data (e.g. % crown cover, or a point to locate on the Holdridge triangle): state the resulting classification and the specific data feature that determines it.

### Use dichotomous keys to identify and classify organisms

**What it means** — A dichotomous key is a series of paired (dichotomous = "two-part") either/or statements about observable features, each choice leading either to another pair of statements or to an identification.

**Why it matters** — Exam questions supply an unfamiliar key and specimen description and ask you to work through it — this tests careful reading under time pressure more than biology content.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Identify* [1–2 marks]: apply a given key to a described organism and state each step number used to reach the final identification, not just the final answer, if the question asks you to show your path.

### Identify the major taxa in the Linnaean system and explain how it is used to classify and name species

**What it means** — Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species (mnemonic: "Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup"). Binomial nomenclature names a species *Genus species* (genus capitalised, species lower case, both italicised).

**Why it matters** — This underpins the taxonomy questions across the whole subject, and connects directly to the SHE dot point below about the Linnaean system's limitations.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Identify* [1 mark] a taxon from a given classification table; *explain* [2 marks] why binomial nomenclature avoids the ambiguity of common names.

### Appreciate that methods of classification are directly related to the purpose for which the data will be used

**What it means** — The Linnaean system organises species hierarchically so scientists can infer similarities and communicate about biodiversity efficiently — but because it was originally built mainly on physical features, its groupings don't always match true evolutionary relatedness, and species get reclassified as new evidence (especially genetic evidence) arrives.

**Why it matters** — This is a "science as a human endeavour" dot point: it's tested through your ability to explain *why* science changes its mind, not through more content recall. It's commonly under-revised because it feels like an aside — it isn't; SHE ideas appear as standalone exam questions.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Explain* [2–3 marks]: "Explain why a species might be reclassified after new genetic data becomes available." The expected answer links the *purpose* of classification (organising/communicating data) to why physical-feature-based groupings can be revised.

### Appreciate that developments in software, computing and supercomputing have been important in ecological classification

**What it means** — Supercomputing enables classification of ecosystems from very large biotic/abiotic datasets, comparison of data over time, and complex modelling that infers evolutionary relationships between species from data collected across many individuals and locations.

**Why it matters** — Another SHE dot point, testing whether you understand *why* modern computing changed what's possible in ecology, not just that it exists.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Explain* [2 marks]: how supercomputing has changed ecological classification compared to earlier physical-feature-only methods — the expected link is scale (more data, more individuals, more locations) enabling patterns humans couldn't compute by hand.

### Appreciate that there are multiple definitions for species, and each has limitations

**What it means** — Beyond the biological species concept: **phylogenetic** (based on shared ancestry/evolutionary lineage), **ecological** (based on the niche a population occupies), **morphological** (based on physical appearance) species concepts — each useful for different organisms or questions, each limited in different situations.

**Why it matters** — This dot point is what makes the earlier "biological species concept and its limitations" dot point make sense as a *pattern* rather than an isolated fact — every species concept trades off some cases for others.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Compare* [3 marks]: given a scenario where the biological species concept fails (e.g. an asexual organism), identify a more appropriate concept and justify why it applies better here.

### Investigate how abiotic factors affect distribution/abundance; changes along an environmental gradient; how environmental factors affect global ecosystem distribution; how classifying ecosystems allows effective management

**What it means** — This is a Science Inquiry dot point: designing or critiquing an actual investigation, not just recalling content. An environmental gradient is a continuous change in an abiotic factor across space (e.g. altitude, distance from a water source) — species composition typically changes continuously along it (zonation) rather than in sharp boundaries.

**Why it matters** — Inquiry dot points get examined through data-response and experimental-design questions — "identify the independent/dependent variable", "identify a confounding variable", "suggest an improvement to this method" — the same skills as Unit 3 Methods' statistics topic, applied to ecology.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Analyse* [3–4 marks], given a described gradient study: identify the independent variable (position along the gradient), the dependent variable (species composition/abundance), and explain the pattern using biotic/abiotic reasoning from earlier dot points.

### Compare species diversity in two spatially variant ecosystems of the same classification

**What it means** — Two ecosystems can carry the *same* classification label (e.g. both "closed forest" under Specht's system) while differing in species diversity — classification by structure doesn't guarantee equal diversity.

**Why it matters** — This dot point directly tests whether you can apply the Simpson's diversity index and richness/evenness dot points to a genuine comparison, tying several earlier dot points together in one question — a common exam pattern for the final dot points of a topic.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Compare* [4 marks], given SDI or richness/evenness data for two same-classification ecosystems: state both values, then explain what the difference implies about each ecosystem (e.g. dominance by one species vs. an even spread).

### Use stratified sampling to identify different habitats/strata within an ecosystem; investigate abiotic and community-composition changes between strata; infer species interactions; classify an ecosystem

**What it means** — Strata are distinct vertical or horizontal layers within one ecosystem (e.g. canopy, understorey, shrub layer, forest floor in a rainforest). Stratified sampling means sampling *each* stratum separately rather than treating the whole ecosystem as uniform, because abiotic conditions (light, moisture, temperature) and species composition genuinely differ between strata.

**Why it matters** — This is the applied, multi-layer version of the sampling dot point earlier — it's usually the most substantial single question in this topic because it draws on distribution patterns, sampling method choice, biotic/abiotic factors and classification all at once.

**What a question on this looks like** — *Investigate/analyse* [5–6 marks], often a multi-part question walking through: identify why stratified (not random) sampling suits a layered ecosystem → describe how abiotic factors would be expected to differ between two named strata → infer a species interaction (e.g. competition for light) that the data suggests.

## Where marks get lost

- **Richness vs evenness, and "method" vs "technique"** — the two most confused vocabulary pairs in this topic. If a question uses one specific term, use that exact term back.
- **Simpson's index arithmetic** — forgetting to sum $n(n-1)$ across *every* species before dividing by $N(N-1)$, or mixing up $n$ (one species) and $N$ (total).
- **"Compare" answered as two descriptions** — write paired points, not parallel paragraphs.
- **"Interpret" answered without citing the actual data given** — a true general statement about biology that never quotes the numbers in front of you doesn't score as interpretation.
- **Treating capture-recapture as pure arithmetic** — the Lincoln index's assumptions are frequently the harder, higher-mark part of the question.
- **Skipping SHE content because it "isn't real biology"** — it's assessed exactly like everything else, and it's often under-revised, which makes it a reliable place to gain marks relative to how little study time it needs.

## Check yourself

1. A community has 5 species with one making up 85% of individuals, and another community has the same 5 species spread evenly. Which has higher evenness, and why?
*The evenly spread community — evenness measures how equally individuals are distributed across species, independent of richness, and both communities have the same richness (5).*

2. What's the difference between a sampling *method* and a sampling *technique*?
*Method is how you choose where to sample (random, systematic, stratified); technique is the physical tool you use to sample (quadrat, transect, capture-recapture).*

3. A population shows a J-curve for the first few generations after colonising a new island, then flattens out. Name both phases and explain what causes the second one.
*Exponential growth, then logistic growth — the flattening happens as the population approaches carrying capacity and density-dependent limiting factors (competition, food, disease) slow the growth rate.*

4. In the Lincoln index, what does it mean for the estimate if marked individuals are more likely to be eaten by predators after release?
*The estimate would be biased — fewer marked individuals survive to be recaptured, making $m$ artificially low, which inflates the estimated population size $N$ above the true value.*

5. Why can't the biological species concept be applied to a fossil?
*The biological species concept relies on observing whether individuals can interbreed and produce fertile offspring, which cannot be tested in extinct organisms known only from fossil remains.*
